## Why the Moon has seasons

The Moon's equator is tilted only about 1.54° to the ecliptic (Cassini's laws), so the subsolar point moves between about -1.59° and +1.59° selenographic latitude in 2026–2030 (ephemeris; the extra beyond 1.54° is physical libration and the Sun's direction seen from the Moon rather than from Earth). At the equator that changes almost nothing. Near the poles the Sun skims the horizon, so a ±1.5° shift decides whether a rim or peak clears the surrounding terrain: this is the lunar season.

Spacing of the southern-summer solstices: 347.8, 347.2, 346.1, 345.6 days (mean 346.7 d). The lunar seasonal cycle is shorter than an Earth year (it follows the eclipse year, about 346.6 d, because the Moon's orbit precesses), so the polar 'best months' come earlier in the calendar every year.

## Method

- Calendar months 2026-01 … 2030-12 (UTC), 60 months tiling the 43,824 ephemeris hours exactly.
- Per month: `core.metrics(site, i0, i1)` with hourly samples — sun = solar disc ≥ 50 % above the LOLA horizon mask, Earth = Earth centre above the mask, both = both at the same hour, longest dark = longest not-sunlit run inside the month (runs are cut at month boundaries).
- A calendar month (28–31 d) is not a whole lunar day (29.53 d), so at the equator a 31-day month can hold one more day than night or the reverse. To separate that calendar effect from the season, each month also gets the same metrics over 709 h (one synodic month) centred on the month (`lunation`). The chart draws the calendar-month bars and the lunation line.
- 'Sun south' months = month-mean subsolar latitude ≤ −1°; 'Sun north' ≥ +1°. 16 and 15 of the 60 months qualify.
